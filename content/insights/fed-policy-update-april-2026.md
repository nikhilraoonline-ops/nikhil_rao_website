The Federal Reserve's April 2026 FOMC meeting concluded with no changes to the federal funds rate, which remains at 5.25–5.50%. While the statement maintained a cautious tone, subtle shifts in language provided some relief to markets.

---

## Key Decisions

- **No rate changes**: The Fed Funds rate held steady at 5.25–5.50%
- **Unchanged balance sheet runoff**: QT continues at the current pace
- **Forward guidance**: Emphasis on data dependency and patience

---

## Statement Analysis

The FOMC statement noted that "inflation has made further progress toward the Committee's 2 percent objective" but cautioned that "the process of getting inflation sustainably to 2 percent has a long way to go."

Notably absent was the previous language about being "prepared to raise rates further if appropriate," signaling a shift toward a more patient approach.

---

## Powell's Press Conference

Chairman Jerome Powell reinforced the dovish pivot during the post-meeting press conference:

- Acknowledged that inflation is "much closer" to target than previously thought
- Emphasized that rate decisions will be based on incoming data
- Downplayed the significance of recent hot CPI prints as "transitory"
- Maintained that the Fed is not on a predetermined path for cuts

---

## Market Reaction

Equity markets rallied on the news, with the S&P 500 gaining over 1% in post-meeting trading. Bond yields declined sharply, with the 10Y UST dropping 13bps.

The VIX fell below 16, indicating reduced volatility expectations.

---

## Implications for Financial Markets

1. **Equities**: Supportive environment for risk assets as rate cut expectations remain intact
2. **Fixed Income**: Continued downward pressure on yields, potential for further curve steepening
3. **FX**: USD weakness likely to persist against major currencies
4. **Credit**: Investment grade spreads may narrow further

---

## Looking Ahead

The next FOMC meeting is scheduled for June 2026. Key data points to watch include upcoming CPI, employment reports, and GDP figures. Markets will be particularly sensitive to any signs of inflation resurgence or labor market weakness.

The Fed's patient approach suggests that the first rate cut may come later in 2026, barring any significant economic surprises.
